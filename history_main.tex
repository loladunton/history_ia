\section{Section A}

This investigation will explore the religious and socio-economic history, outlined by the research question “To what extent was Hong Xiuquan’s religious ideology the primary cause of the outbreak of the Taiping rebellion, between 1800-1855?”.

The document of the Taiping Kingdom, called the “The Land System of the Heavenly Kingdom”, is relevant to this investigation as it was originally published in 1853 and written by Hong Xiuquan, the leader of the Taiping Rebellion. This source offers direct insight into the religious motivation that prompted the Taiping rebellion. Secondly, a letter, written in 1839, from the Qing Dynasty Scholar and high imperial commissioner, Lin Zexu, to Queen Victoria, is of great significance as it documents the socioeconomic devastation caused by the opium trade, and corruption.


\subsection{An excerpt from “The Land System of the Heavenly Kingdom” document published by Hong Xiuquan, the leader of the Taiping Rebellion in 1853}

A value of the origin of the basic founding document, is that, as a primary source from the time of the event written by the leader of the rebellion, it provides direct and reliable evidence of Xiuquan’s ideology and thinking. A value of the purpose to influence the public, spread the word of God and gain widespread support shows how Xiuquan aimed to share his beliefs as part of of his mission as well as to lead society in his, new direction. Finally, a value of the content is that by displaying the rules that would govern a Taiping society, it shows the underlying issues present at the time that Xiuquan and his followers felt needed to be addressed through rebellion.

Additionally this source also displays a limitation of the origin, as having been written by Xiuquan himself, it excludes the general public’s response to the document and the imposing ideology. A limitation of the purpose to persuade followers and Chinese citizens means this document is particularly crafted to be appealing to the wide public, resulting in key information intentionally being left out, such as the role of violence in trying to implement the policy outlined in this source. Finally, a limitation of the content that outlines the radical policies Xiuquan and his followers wished to implement, is that these policies were largely not implemented in practice, therefore limiting the historian’s accurate knowledge of subsequent developments.

\subsection{Letter from high imperial commissioner, Lin Zexu, to Queen Victoria in 1839}

A value of the origin of this source being written by a powerful and respected member of the Qing Dynasty at the time means that he would have a deep understanding of the socioeconomic landscape he details in his letter from his working experience, therefore adding reliability to his statements. A value of the purpose to convince Queen Victoria to halt the opium drug trade is how the desperation with which Lin writes the letter shows the extent of widespread socioeconomic hardship and damage to society has been caused by the opium trade, contextualising the suffering of the public for a historian. A value of the content would be how it shows the confidence and proactiveness with which Commissioner Lin responded to the opium trade - with a defined ultimatum hinting towards the death penalty for opium merhcants and confronting Queen Victoria with the hypocrisy of the opium trade.

A limitation of the origin would be the implication that Commissioner Lin speaks on behalf of the entire Chinese population, while some individuals, such as opium merchants, might feel differently to his perspective. A limitation of the purpose to persuade Queen Victoria is that the resulting effect of such a strongly worded letter is not revealed, limiting a historian’s knowledge of the outcome. Finally, a limitation of the content would be that it predominantly focuses on how the opium trade damaged Chinese society at the time, disregarding numerous other factors, such as extreme population growth, corruption, and inflation.

\section{Section B}

The arguments surrounding the significance of factors that led to the outbreak of the Taiping Rebellion are highly contested amongst historians. The Taiping Rebellion began in 1850 with the mobilisation of the God Worshipping Society under Hong Xiuquan, followed by the official proclamation of the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom the next year. Thereafter, 14 years of bloody civil war would ensue resulting in the death of 20 to 30 million people on the conservative end (however some estimates reach up to 70 million deaths), making the Taiping Rebellion the deadliest civil war in history. Despite the complexity amidst historians’ debate, one common thread is the general agreement upon national unrest prompting mobilisation which then caused rebellion, however such consensus’ diverge when the motivation for such unrest is unpacked further (Ch9, Leung, Hyndman) . Therefore this investigation will use a range of secondary sources to explore the factors that prompted initial discontent, which can be most aptly categorised into external economic, religious and internal socio-political factors.
